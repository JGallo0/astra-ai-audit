Key terms and concepts 
3
Biochar
Biochar is a stable organic carbon compound formed through pyrolysis.
Biochar Decay Rate
Decay rate refers to the speed at which biochar’s stored carbon is released back into the atmosphere due to 
environmental and external factors such as soil conditions and temperature, application methods, and 
natural risks.
Biochar Stability
Refers to the inherent ability of biochar to retain sequestered carbon over time, based on its intrinsic 
characteristics and resistance to chemical, biological, and physical degradation.
Carbon credit
A tradable unit representing one metric ton of carbon dioxide (CO2), or an equivalent amount of another 
greenhouse gas (GHG), avoided or removed from Earth’s atmosphere.
Gasification
A thermochemical conversion process that heats biomass at high temperatures (800–1,000°C) with a 
controlled amount of oxygen or steam, producing syngas and a smaller quantity of biochar than pyrolysis.
Greenhouse Gas (GHG) 
Emissions
Refers to the release of gases into the atmosphere that trap heat and contribute to climate change.
Hydrogen to Carbon 
Ratio (H/Corg)
The hydrogen (H) to organic carbon(Corg) molar ratio correlates with the stability of biochar. 
Internal Rate of Return 
(IRR)
Internal Rate of Return used for investment analysis to estimate the return on an investment.
Life Cycle Assessment 
(LCA)
This refers to the analysis conducted to estimate the amount of emissions associated with the sourcing of 
biomass, the creation of biochar and its deployment.
Over-crediting risk
This refers to the risk that the project has issued credits in excess of what is justiﬁable against the business 
as usual scenario.
Organic Carbon (Corg) 
The organic fraction of total carbon in biochar, measured as a percentage of dry weight. It is calculated as 
total carbon minus inorganic carbon content.
Permanence 
Adjustment Factor
This refers to the percentage of carbon that will stay secure in the biochar for the next 200+  years. 
Project emissions
Emissions associated with ongoing operations of the carbon credit project.
Pyrolysis
Pyrolysis is the heating/burning of an element in an oxygen-absent, controlled environment.
Random Reflectance
A measure of the optical reﬂectivity of carbon structures under microscopic analysis, derived from coal 
petrology. Higher values indicate greater thermal maturity and aromaticity, which are associated with 
increased long-term carbon stability.
Vintage
This refers to the year, or timeframe, associated with an issued carbon credit.

What is a Biochar project? 
5
Biochar projects produce removals credits through the thermochemical conversion of biomass, such as pyrolysis and 
gasiﬁcation, in an oxygen-limited environment. Not all carbon is converted to biochar during the thermochemical  
process; co-products include bio-oil, pyrolysis gases, and heat. The biochar produced is abundant in carbon, stable and 
has numerous applications. Its stability is primarily determined by the production temperature and the resulting H/Corg 
ratio.
The input to the process is primarily plant-based and animal-derived biomass. The biomass can be sourced locally from 
the surrounding forests or agricultural lands and may come from many sources, for example a forest management 
company with wood chips or a farmer with grass and crop residue. The sourced biomass has a direct impact on the quality 
of the biochar created. This input typically requires pre-treatment, including drying, before entering the thermochemical 
conversion process. 
The reactor heats the biomass in an oxygen-limited environment at temperatures typically ranging from 300°C to 800°C. 
The process creates other co-products such as bio-oil, syngas and excess thermal energy, which can be sold for 
additional revenue streams. Alternative revenue streams, in addition to those derived from the sale of carbon credits, 
have meaningful implications for the additionality of biochar projects as these revenue streams impact the extent to 
which carbon ﬁnance is required for these projects to be economic.

Biochar is predominantly applied to soil, where it improves soil fertility through enhanced nutrient retention, water 
holding capacity, and microbial activity. Innovative non-soil applications, such as incorporation into building materials, are 
emerging as alternative carbon storage pathways.
Current scientiﬁc consensus primarily determines biochar decay rates using H/Corg ratio and ambient soil temperature 
as key parameters. Emerging approaches such as random reﬂectance propose longer durability claims (1000+ years), 
though the science remains contested and unstandardized. Over-crediting risk arises when a project’s reported removals 
exceed what consensus-based permanence models indicate for equivalent conditions, or when reported input data fails 
cross-validation against independently veriﬁed sources. Traceability from feedstock to end-use is also essential to verify 
that credited removals are real and permanent.

To evaluate net carbon removals, Sylvera has developed an in-house Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) model, focusing on key 
emission sources including feedstock sourcing, energy consumption, and construction. In the updated framework 
(February 2026), the LCA impact on the carbon score is now calculated proportionally to the entire removal, rather than 
as a direct deduction. This means that individual emission  variations have a smaller impact on the overall carbon score, 
providing a more representative assessment of a project’s total GHG sources, sinks and reservoirs. Sylvera’s LCA model 
has been peer-reviewed by UCL Consultants, and uses the most up-to-date emission factors available from ecoinvent, 
UK Government and academic literature. 
Emission calculations are performed by taking activity data, matching this to an emission factor, and multiplying these 
together to give an output in CO2 equivalent (CO2e):
Activity data * Emission Factor = kgCO2e
Therefore, by performing these calculations across important emissions sources, Sylvera can assess whether the project 
is likely to be accurately reporting its GHG emissions.

Sylvera’s additionality score assesses whether (1) a project would or wouldn’t have been implemented in the absence 
of revenue generated through the sale of carbon credits, (2) the project is not mandatory/strongly supported by 
policies and regulations and (3) the project is not presenting double accounting risk with other VCM activities.
Additionality underpins the validity of credits issued by a project. If the project is not additional, then one credit 
purchased does not equate to one metric ton of carbon removed and therefore yields no climate beneﬁt above the 
business-as-usual (BAU) scenario.
A project would score high in additionality if Sylvera’s ﬁnancial analysis demonstrates the need for carbon ﬁnance to 
make the project economic. This is not a binary test and the degree of additionality depends on the carbon price 
required to make it economic. On the other hand, a project would score low in additionality if the revenues from biochar 
and any possible co-products are enough alone to make the project economically viable.

Financial Additionality
The assessment of ﬁnancial additionality involves determining whether the economic viability of the project relies on 
carbon revenues. Sylvera examines if the carbon credits revenue bridges the economic viability gap of the project 
through sensitivity analysis. Biochar projects that generate additional revenue streams by selling biochar or 
co-products to third parties, Sylvera assesses whether the project would still be economically viable without carbon 
revenues. If a project is economically feasible without carbon credits, the credits are considered non-additional. This 
ensures that carbon ﬁnance is allocated only to projects that require it to proceed.
Biochar projects can have more than one revenue stream. The biochar itself can be sold at prices based on the quality 
of the biochar and other transaction parameters. Furthermore, pyrolysis also creates thermal energy that can be sold 
along with any bio-oils produced during the process. These products can create signiﬁcant revenues for the project 
crossing the hurdle rate required to make the project economic. If these revenues are not enough, carbon ﬁnance 
might be required to bridge this gap. However, reliance on high carbon credit prices can make the likelihood of 
achieving an IRR above the hurdle rate more diicult and unlikely. 
A high-quality project demonstrates that carbon ﬁnance is necessary for the project to exceed its hurdle rate, while 
requiring only a modest carbon price to do so. The project should also account for all emissions associated with 
producing biochar and its co-products, including bio-oil and energy, and allocate them properly across outputs.
How does Sylvera assess additionality?
Policy & Regulatory Landscape
Sylvera considers the policy and regulatory context to assess additionality. Projects are evaluated to conﬁrm they are 
not mandated by law or regulation and to identify whether subsidies or government funding diminish their 
additionality. By integrating any provided subsidies or ﬁnancial incentives into our economic analysis, Sylvera ensures 
that carbon credits are issued only where they play a critical role in making the project viable. This safeguards the 
integrity of credits in regions with supportive policy landscapes.
Double Accounting
To address the risk of double accounting, research are performed to ensure that carbon credits are not claimed by 
multiple stakeholders. In biochar projects, this involves verifying that the producer and end user of the biochar do not 
both claim the same carbon beneﬁt. Clear documentation of credit allocation is required to establish ownership and 
avoid duplication. This approach safeguards the integrity and credibility of the issued credits.

Permanence refers to the risk that the sequestered carbon will later be reversed and released back into the atmosphere. 
The input of climatic variables, record of past events, project speciﬁc conditions and mitigative activities are used to 
inform the permanence risk scoring. Permanence is conceptualized as a scale that distinguishes the relative degree of 
non-permanence (or reversal) risk between projects. 
For soil application, the permanence of carbon stored in the biochar depends primarily on the H/C ratio and ambient soil 
temperature, with production temperature acting as an indirect inﬂuence through its eect on biochar quality. Soil type, 
location, and climatic conditions further shape durability: biochar decays faster in tropical climates due to higher soil 
temperatures, while clay soils can enhance permanence through physical encapsulation within soil aggregates, though 
high soil moisture may oset this by increasing microbial activity.  Projects making claims of 1,000 years or longer via 
random reﬂectance are treated with additional scrutiny, as this method remains scientiﬁcally contested and 
unstandardized (Sanei et al., 2025; Drobniak et al., 2026). 
For non-soil applications, the permanence is assessed based on the expected lifespan and end-of-life traceability. This 
involves examining how these dierent applications inﬂuence the durability and eectiveness of biochar in retaining 
carbon. Use cases such as construction materials oer strong physical protection during their service life, though 
end-of-life disposal introduces reversal risk. Applications such as water ﬁltration carry higher near-term reversal risk, as 
biochar may degrade during active use.
Biochar credits have a low permanence risk given the overall high stability of biochar. The only case where biochar 
stability is uncertain is where there is a high likelihood and high severity of ﬁre events, however in comparison to 
nature-based projects, there is signiﬁcantly less risk of reversal. As a result, Sylvera places greater emphasis on 
anthropogenic risk, focusing on whether the end-use location is traceable and whether there is clear evidence that 
biochar has been applied.
